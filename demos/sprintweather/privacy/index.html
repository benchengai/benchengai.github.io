Privacy model

What SprintWeather stores, what it shows, and the rules in between. The full write-up, with the evaluation, is in docs/privacy-model.md.

Self-report, not emotion inference

The EU AI Act, Article 5(1)(f), prohibits:

the placing on the market, the putting into service for this specific purpose, or the use of AI systems to infer emotions of a natural person in the areas of workplace and education institutions, except where the use of the AI system is intended to be put in place or into the market for medical or safety reasons;

Article 3(39) defines the related term used elsewhere in the Act:

‘emotion recognition system’ means an AI system for the purpose of identifying or inferring emotions or intentions of natural persons on the basis of their biometric data;

SprintWeather is built so that no AI system infers anyone's emotions. People report their own energy, workload and goal clarity on a scale they choose; the app adds them up. The one model step summarises what the notes say about the work into team-level themes; its instructions forbid adding feelings the writers did not state or describing any one person, and it never sees who wrote what. No biometric data is collected.

This describes the design. It is not legal advice and makes no claim about whether a particular deployment complies with the AI Act or the GDPR; a team using it at work still has to assess that, for example whether participation is really voluntary.

What is stored

  • Per response: sprint, three 1–5 scores, blocked yes/no, optional role and sub-team, optional note.
  • No name, member id, code or timestamp on a response, and responses are kept in random order.
  • Check-in codes only as hashes, in a separate list, so a stored response cannot be matched to its code.
  • Notes only until themes are made for their sprint (or the facilitator discards them).

Because no response carries an identity, per-person trends cannot be computed, by anyone.

The release rules

  1. Nothing while check-in is open. Otherwise you could compare the figures before and after one more person answers.
  2. At least k responses behind every figure. k defaults to 5; it can be raised, but values below 3 are refused, not rounded up.
  3. No differencing. Showing “Payments” next to the team total also reveals “everyone not in Payments” by subtraction. So a group is shown only if every atom stays at 0 or at least k responses. An atom is a set of responses that are in exactly the same shown groups. Any figure that can be worked out by adding and subtracting shown figures, across groups, across role and sub-team, with the team total, or across sprints, is a combination of atoms, so none covers fewer than k responses. People who chose “prefer not to say” form an atom too.
  4. Multi-sprint figures pool whole released sprints only (the before/after comparison for action items), so they never split an atom.
  5. k and the groups are frozen when a sprint closes. Re-computing a published sprint with a different k could produce a second version that differs from the first by a small group.
  6. Hidden means no numbers, not even a response count, and the label is the same whatever the reason.

Themes without anyone's wording

  1. Links, emails, @handles, ticket ids and numbers are removed; notes are shuffled and given throwaway ids. The model never sees roles, sub-teams or scores.
  2. The model proposes themes and must cite the notes behind each one. The output schema only accepts ids that were in the prompt.
  3. A second call checks, theme by theme, which cited notes really make that point. Only confirmed notes count, and a theme needs at least k of them. The checker can remove citations, never add them.
  4. A deterministic check rejects any statement that reuses a word or a four-word phrase that only one note used (beyond everyday words), or a name fewer than k notes used. Rejected statements get up to two rewrites; if they still fail, the theme is withheld.
  5. Only the statement, its topic, its polarity and its count are kept. Then the notes are deleted.

So a theme can only say what at least k people said, in words that are not traceable to one of them. Themes are made for the whole team only, never per group, and the retro-question step sees only what this page would show.

What this does not protect against

  • Homogeneity: if everyone in a shown group gives the same answer, the aggregate reveals what each of them said.
  • Outside knowledge: who was away, who joined, who usually writes about what.
  • A dishonest operator: whoever runs the server can read the store, or use unused codes to add fake responses until a small group reaches k.
  • Inference from trends: if only one person joined between two sprints, a change in the average hints at their answer, though it never equals it.
Research basis

SprintWeather is grounded in the systematic mapping study Emotions in Software Engineering: A Systematic Mapping Study (57 studies, classified by five emotion categories and five SDLC phases). Its Team Health & Well-being category has 16 studies, and none of them is about maintenance:

  • The study finds the field has become good at detecting emotional strain but has made little progress in designing and evaluating interventions: only two team-health studies evaluate a concrete tool aimed at improving well-being. SprintWeather is a small intervention for the maintenance phase: a recurring check-in that feeds the team's own retrospective.
  • The study's discussion of the EU AI Act names what remains open: aggregate and anonymised team-level signals, and strictly opt-in tools. SprintWeather stays inside that space by using voluntary self-report only.
  • Closest prior work: Grassi et al. (2023) support emotion awareness in retrospectives with biometric wristbands; SprintWeather asks instead. Kuutila et al. (2018) show that repeated self-report (experience sampling) can track well-being over months.
  • The privacy rules build on k-anonymity (Sweeney, 2002), on the classic differencing (“tracker”) attacks on query-size restrictions (Denning et al., 1979), and on the known homogeneity limit of k-anonymity (Machanavajjhala et al., 2007).

Limitations
  • Tested on synthetic data only: no real team has used SprintWeather, and the theme evaluation uses hand-written notes with planted themes. The numbers say how the pipeline behaves on that data, not how useful it is to a team.
  • Self-report has known biases: people who are struggling may not check in, and answers can be shaded towards what feels safe to say.
  • k-anonymity hides who said what, not what a whole group said: if every member of a shown group gives the same answer, the aggregate reveals it. Someone with outside knowledge (who was on holiday, who joined) can still narrow things down.
  • Before/after comparisons for action items are observational. Responses cannot be linked across sprints (by design), so the interval treats them as independent, and other changes in the same sprints are not controlled for.
  • LLM themes can miss points, merge them, or be withheld by the support and wording checks; a withheld theme is lost signal. The wording check relies on a hand-made list of everyday words.
  • Whoever runs the server can read the raw store and could submit fake responses with unused codes to lift a small group over k. The guarantees are about what the app shows, not about a dishonest operator. Local mode has no login.
  • This is not legal advice; whether a deployment complies with the AI Act or the GDPR is for its operator to assess.

Self-report only: SprintWeather never infers anyone's emotions and never shows an individual's answers.
